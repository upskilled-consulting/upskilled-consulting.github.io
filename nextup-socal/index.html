Where we left off · June 5

In June we mapped the levels.
Today: what each one costs to run.

  • Level 1 — Assistant. One prompt, one answer. Cheap enough that nobody checks.
  • Level 2 — Collaborator. The tool knows your context. More reading, more tokens.
  • Level 3 — Chief of Staff. It works without you. It reads constantly.
Where today picks up

74% of companies plan to deploy agentic AI within two years. So the useful question now is a budgeting one: what does Level 3 cost to run for a year?

Callback slide. Get nods here — most of the room was on the June 5 call.

Beat 1–3: walk the three levels quickly. Say: "each level up, the tool reads more on your behalf. That's the whole cost story in one sentence."

Beat 4: land the question. Do not answer it yet. "Hold that number in your head. We'll have it in twenty minutes."

If asked "is agentic worth it?" — say: yes, often. That's exactly why the cost needs to be deliberate instead of accidental.

02 · The multiplier

Nobody's budget breaks on price.
It breaks on volume.

Transition slide. This is the hinge of the whole talk — take the pause.

Beat 1–4: assemble the equation one term at a time. "Cost per task — we just did that, it's tiny. Tasks per day. People. Working days."

"Everyone in your organization is looking at the first term. The invoice is made of the other three."

Set up the next three slides: "Same company — 600 people, 250 working days — three different ways of using the same tool."

Scenario 1 of 3 · Level 1

The assistant.
Chat, summarize, draft.

This is the shape they'll see three times. Teach them to read it here, then it's fast.

Beat 1: 20,000 input tokens a day. "Fifteen thousand words read. That's a heavy user."

Beat 2: 5,000 output. Note output is the smaller number but nearly two-thirds of the cost — the 8× asymmetry showing up.

Beat 3: seven and a half cents a day, per person.

Beat 4: $11,250 a year for 600 people. "Eighteen dollars and seventy-five cents per employee per year. Cheaper than the coffee. This is the number people quote when they say AI is cheap."

Scenario 2 of 3 · Levels 2–3

The agent.
Agents read far more than they write.

Beat 1: 500,000 input tokens a day — 25× the assistant. "That's not a bigger document. That's the same documents, read over and over, every turn of the loop." (Flag: we come back to exactly this on the context slide.)

Beat 2: output barely moves — 20,000. The agent isn't writing more; it's reading more.

Beat 3: the cost flips. Input is now the majority of the bill. Everything people learned about "output is expensive" stops applying.

Beat 4–5: $123,750/year. Eleven times scenario one, for the same headcount.

"Nothing about the price list changed. The tool just started reading on your behalf."

Scenario 3 of 3 · Past Level 3

Always-on automation.
Agents that never stop watching.

Multiple autonomous agents, continuously monitoring — inboxes, tickets, inventory, pricing, compliance.

Beat 1: 2 million input tokens per employee per day. "A hundred times the assistant. And notice — nobody typed any of it."

Beat 4–5: $525,000/year.

"This is the number I want in your head when a vendor says 'and it runs continuously in the background.' That sentence has a price."

Important honesty beat: this can absolutely be worth it. A half-million in inference that replaces three million in labor is a good trade. The failure mode isn't spending — it's not knowing.

03 · The levers

Five levers. Same capability,
a fraction of the bill.

Be explicit that this is a model, not a measurement. Say it before the first bar drops — it buys you credibility for the rest of the section.

Each lever gets its own slide next. Here, just name them and let the bar fall:

  • Right-size the model — stop paying frontier prices for routine work.
  • Script the deterministic steps — don't rent judgment you don't need.
  • Engineer the context — stop re-reading the same page 250 times.
  • Stop redoing work — save the artifact, not the conversation.
  • Run commodity work locally — smaller models, your hardware, your data.

Final beat: $525K → roughly $147K. "Three and a half times cheaper, and I haven't taken a single capability away from anyone."

Lever 1 · Right-size the model

We’re paying frontier prices
for a 1.7% edge.

Beat 1: the gap. Best open-weight models closed from 8% to 1.7% behind the best paid models in a single year.

Beat 2: the routing split. "Of a hundred tasks, how many genuinely need the best model on earth? Be honest."

Beat 3: the arithmetic. Route 80% of tasks to a tier that's 10× cheaper → you pay 28% of what you paid. A 72% cut, on the first day, from a routing rule.

Beat 4: the real point — defaulting to the premium model for every task is a tax on habit, not a quality decision.

Objection to pre-empt: "won't quality drop?" Answer: on the 20% that matters, you still use the best model. That's what routing means. Measure it — pick 50 real tasks, run both, compare.

Lever 2 · Script the deterministic steps

Most of what you're automating
doesn't need to think.

This is the least popular lever and the most valuable one. Lead with the honesty: where the work needs judgment or language, the model wins and you should pay for it.

Beat 1–2: the two lines. A model call has no economy of scale — every run costs the same as the first. A script has almost nothing but economies of scale.

Beat 3: the crossover — around 3,200 runs a year. "That's thirteen runs a working day. One recurring report."

Beat 4: at 150,000 runs: $75,000 vs $1,600.

"If the task has the same shape every time — pull the file, reformat, check the threshold, send it — you are renting a Ph.D. to read a thermometer."

Practical test to give them: if you can write down the rule, you don't need a model to follow it.

Lever 3 · Engineer the context

Your agent is re-reading the same page
all day long.

The payoff for the flag you planted on scenario 2. This is the most technical slide — keep it to the shape, not the mechanism.

Beat 1: the naive loop. Turn 1 sends 2,000 tokens. Turn 2 re-sends turn 1 plus the new part. Turn 20 re-sends everything.

Beat 2: the curve. It's not linear — it's quadratic. Twenty turns of a small conversation is 420,000 tokens.

Beat 3: the engineered line — rolling window plus a summary. 120,000 tokens for the same work. 3.5× less.

Beat 4: and prompt caching cuts the repeated prefix further on most platforms — ask your vendor what their cache discount is; it's usually the single easiest call you'll make.

"Your agent's context window is a shopping cart nobody empties."

Lever 4 · Stop redoing work

The chat window is a shredder
with a very good memory of nothing.

Beat 1: the sessions grid. Every dot is somebody solving something in a chat window.

Beat 2: the red dots — work that was already done, by someone else, last month, in a session nobody kept. "Chat sessions are great for one-off tasks. But the output is almost never saved as an artifact."

Beat 3: the cost. At 30% rework on a $525K bill, that's $157,500 a year to re-answer answered questions — and that's before you count the salary time.

The fix is unglamorous and free: put the prompts and the outputs in version control. A prompt someone tuned for four hours is an asset. Right now it lives in one person's scrollback.

Tie to June 5: this is what turns a Level 1 individual habit into a Level 2 team capability.

Lever 5 · Run commodity work locally

Smaller models, your hardware,
nothing leaves the building.

Beat 1: quantization in one sentence — "you store each number in the model with less precision. The model gets smaller and faster; on routine work you can barely tell." An 8-billion-parameter model goes from 16 GB to 4 GB.

Beat 2: what that unlocks — 4 GB runs on a laptop. Not a data center. A laptop.

Beat 3: break-even. Fixed hardware cost versus a monthly API line that never stops.

Beat 4: and the part that isn't about money at all — no data leaves the system. No API call, no third-party retention question, no vendor breach that includes your documents.

For this audience: this is the answer to "legal won't let us put customer data in ChatGPT." It's also, quietly, the cheapest answer.

04 · What you don't spend

What you don't put in the prompt
is a line on the budget too.

Beat 1–2: the flow — everything your team pastes in goes somewhere. Most of it is fine. Four categories are not.

Beat 3: credentials, PII, client IP, internal financials. Each one gets pasted by someone who is trying to be helpful and fast. Nobody in this picture is careless.

Beat 4: the asymmetry. It costs nothing to strip it. It costs a great deal to explain it.

Practical, do-it-this-week version: one page, four bullets, "never paste these." Plus a shared, sanitized prompt template for the two or three workflows everybody actually runs.

Note the second-order cost: fewer tokens in the prompt is also literally a smaller bill. Hygiene and thrift point the same direction here.

06 · Who gets the lever

The most expensive line item
is the one that isn't on the invoice.

Pivot here. Everything so far has been “spend less.” This is “don’t mistake spending less for winning.”

Beat 1: the compounding gap. An hour a day, per person, over a year. The teams that automate pull away from the ones that don't, and the distance grows.

Beat 2 — deliver this carefully, it is the most important slide in the deck for this room. The old story was that women were not using AI. That gap has essentially closed: 50% of men and 47% of women have used a chatbot. Two years ago that gap was eleven points.

What has not closed is intensity. Daily use, 27% against 20%. Using it for work, 40% against 35%. Believing it makes you more productive, 35% against 25%. The door is open. The gap is in how far through it people walk.

If the room is right for it, add the Lean In finding: only 30% of women say their manager encouraged them to use AI, against 37% of men — and men were 27% more likely to be praised for using it. Same behaviour, different credit. That is worth naming out loud in a room like this one.

Beat 3: the tie-back to June 5. The line on screen is Ashika’s closing slide quoted in full — read it as hers, then add your half.

"That was Ashika’s last slide in June. Multiplying your output is the whole game. My one addition: multiply it cheaply, and nobody gets to take it away from you when the invoice lands."

Sources, if challenged: Pew Research Center, 5,119 U.S. adults, fielded Feb 17–23 2026. The workplace and recognition figures are Lean In’s 2026 survey, reported in Forbes. Both are on the slide footnote.

06 · Who gets the lever

The appetite is already there.
The training is the missing line item.

This is the constructive answer to the slide before it. That one showed a gap in intensity. This one shows what actually closes it — and it is not enthusiasm, because the enthusiasm is already there.

Beat 1 — the demand. AI is the number one topic women say they want to learn about. And 63% report a lack of skills and access to training on the job. Say it plainly: "the constraint is not interest. It is access."

Beat 2 — the scale. C-suite executives expect, on average, that 46% of their workforce needs reskilling within three years. Nearly half. That is not an HR programme, that is a budget line — and most of the rooms I sit in have not costed it.

Beat 3 — the tie-back. Every lever in this talk needs somebody who knows to pull it. Routing, context, scripting — none of it happens because you bought a tool. It happens because someone was taught what to look for.

"You are going to spend money on AI this year either way. The cheapest line on the whole page is teaching the people you already employ what these levers are."

For this room specifically: if 46% of the workforce needs reskilling and women already report less access to training, then the reskilling budget is where the adoption gap either closes or gets locked in. That is a decision someone in this audience will personally make.

Where to start

Four moves you can make without a budget request.

01 · Meter it

You cannot manage what you’re not measuring.

Ask for cost per team, per month — and keep building your intuition for cost per workflow.

02 · Route it

Name your three biggest use cases. Work through two of them using the cheaper model.

Run the tasks on the cheaper model for two weeks and compare the output. If no material difference can be discerned, you’ve found savings.

03 · Automate one thing

Pick the task that runs the same way every week.

The Friday report nobody enjoys building. Ask for it to be automated once, and for the recipe to live somewhere the team can find it instead of one person’s inbox.

04 · Budget per outcome

Set a price per thing, not per month.

A monthly cap tells you when you're in trouble; tracking cost per task tells you whether it was worth it.

Close hard on #1 and #4 — those are the two an executive can order without a technical conversation.

If you only get one sentence: "Ask for cost per outcome, not cost per month. Keep building your intuition for cost per task, and bring other departments into that conversation."

Offer the one-pager: "Everything on this slide is in the checklist that goes out with the deck."

Wrapping up

The takeaways worth keeping.

  1. Tokens are cheap. Volume drives the bill. A hundred-page report costs about a stamp; nothing on that chart will ever break a budget.
  2. Agents read far more than they write. As we demonstrated, always-on agents can easily cost 47x more than you’re accustomed to paying for ad hoc chatbot sessions.
  3. Roughly three-quarters of cost is decision-driven, not a set price. Routing, context and scripting get you most of the way back — without removing capabilities.
  4. Ask for cost per outcome, not cost per month. Continue building intuition around cost per task and partnering across departments to begin analyzing monthly spend by task and workflow.
